# *Govind v. State of Madhya Pradesh*

**Court / Bench:** Supreme Court of India; three judges (K. K. Mathew, V. R. Krishna Iyer, P. K. Goswami). Judgment of the Court delivered by Mathew, J.   **Decided:** 18 March 1975   **Citation:** 1975 AIR 1378; 1975 SCR (3) 946; (1975) 2 SCC 148

## Facts

The petitioner, a citizen of India, had a police history sheet opened against him on the basis that he was a habitual criminal, and he was placed under surveillance. He alleged that the police made domiciliary visits by day and night, secretly picketed his house, had the village *patel* watch his movements, and required him to report whenever he left the village. He said that several false cases had been filed against him and that he had been acquitted in all but two. The State replied that he had been convicted in 1962 under ss. 452 and 456 IPC and bound over in 1969, and that he was a dangerous criminal kept under surveillance to prevent offences. He challenged Regulations 855 and 856 of the Madhya Pradesh Police Regulations in a writ petition under Article 32 of the Constitution of India.

## Issues

1. Were Regulations 855 and 856 framed under the Police Act, 1961, so that they have the force of law?
2. Do the surveillance measures in Regulation 856, especially domiciliary visits, violate Art. 21 or Art. 19(1)(d), including any right to privacy?

## Arguments

- **Petitioner:** the Regulations were not framed under any provision of the Police Act; even if they were framed under s. 46(2), domiciliary visits violate Arts. 19(1)(d) and 21; and the right to privacy is itself a fundamental right that the Regulations infringe.
- **Respondent (State):** the petitioner is a dangerous criminal determined to lead a criminal life, and surveillance is needed to prevent him from committing offences.

## Holding

1. Yes. The Regulations were framed under s. 46(2)(c) of the Police Act and have the force of law.
2. No violation. Because Regulation 856 has the force of law and the procedure is reasonable (read with Regulations 853(c) and 857), Art. 21 is not violated. Even assuming a fundamental right to privacy, it is not absolute, and surveillance confined to dangerous persons is a reasonable restriction. The petition was dismissed.

## Reasoning

- **Force of law.** One object of the Police Act is to prevent crime: its preamble refers to "the prevention and detection of crime", and s. 23 makes preventing offences a police duty. Domiciliary visits serve that object by checking whether the person is at home or has gone out to commit offences, so the Regulations fall under the general rule-making power in s. 46(2)(c). This distinguishes *Kharak Singh*, where the regulation was held not to be "law" because of the State's concession that the U.P. Regulations were not framed under the Police Act.
- **Privacy.** The Court reviewed *Kharak Singh* (majority and Subba Rao J.'s minority view), *Griswold v. Connecticut*, *Roe v. Wade*, Warren and Brandeis, and Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights. It held that privacy must develop case by case and, assuming it exists as a fundamental right emanating from Arts. 19(1)(d) and 21, that a law infringing it must satisfy the **compelling state interest** test.
- **Art. 21.** What Art. 21 guarantees is that no one is deprived of life or personal liberty except by procedure established by law, and the procedure here is reasonable.
- **Art. 19(1)(d) and 19(5).** Even if Art. 19(1)(d) includes privacy of movement, Regulation 856 is a reasonable restriction in the public interest.
- **Reading down.** To keep the Regulations within the Constitution, the Court limited surveillance to persons against whom reasonable material shows a determination to lead a life of crime affecting public peace or security. Domiciliary visits must be confined to the clearest cases of danger to community security.

## Ratio decidendi

Police regulations made under s. 46(2)(c) of the Police Act, 1961 have the force of law. Surveillance under them, including domiciliary visits, does not violate Art. 21 or Art. 19(1)(d) when it is read down to persons who are dangerous security risks: even if a fundamental right to privacy exists, it is not absolute and may be restricted by law for a compelling public interest.

The Court only **assumed**, and did not decide, that privacy is a fundamental right. Whether its recognition of privacy forms part of the ratio is therefore open to argument.

## Obiter dicta

- The catalogue of what privacy protects: "the personal intimacies of the home, the family", marriage, motherhood, procreation and child rearing.
- The question of whether enforcing morality can be a compelling state interest, which the Court expressly declined to decide.
- The closing advice that the State should revise these "old police regulations verging perilously near unconstitutionality".

None. The text shows a single judgment of the Court delivered by Mathew, J.

## Place in doctrine

- **Follows and distinguishes** *Kharak Singh v. State of U.P.* [1964] 1 SCR 332. As described in this judgment, the *Kharak Singh* majority struck down domiciliary visits under Regulation 236(b) because there was no "law" behind them, and held the other provisions valid "as no right of privacy has been guaranteed by the Constitution". Subba Rao, J., in the minority, read privacy into Art. 21. The bench strength of *Kharak Singh* is not stated in this text (*to be verified*).
- **Later cases:** the citator information in the text lists 1981 SC 760 and 1982 SC 710; how they treat *Govind* is *to be verified*. The later recognition of privacy as a fundamental right by a nine-judge bench in *K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (2017) is well established but not in the supplied text.
